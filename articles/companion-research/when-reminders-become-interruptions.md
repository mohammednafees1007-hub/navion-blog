# When Does a Helpful Reminder Become an Interruption?

A reminder to take a break may be welcome after finishing a paragraph and frustrating halfway through explaining an idea. For a desk companion concept, the question is not simply whether it can deliver a prompt. It is whether the person wants that prompt at that moment, and how easily they can decline it.

NAVION ROBOTICS is exploring a companion for students, researchers and creators. The project remains in research and concept development, with no companion product available for sale. The scenarios below are proposals for discussion, not implemented features or reported study results. [Current NAVION research overview](https://navionrobotics.com/).

## Consider timing before adding more reminders

Microsoft's Human-AI Experience Toolkit recommends considering the user's task and environment when deciding when an AI system should act or interrupt. Its guidance also calls for attention to privacy when using contextual information. That provides a useful design question for this concept: what information would be necessary to make a reminder welcome? [Microsoft HAX: Time services based on context](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/guideline/time-services-based-on-context/).

A possible starting point for discussion is an explicit choice such as “quiet until I finish this session.” That is a proposed interaction, not evidence that NAVION has built a quiet mode or that the mode improves concentration. Research should also ask what “finish” means to the person using it.

## Compare three versions of the same moment

Imagine someone who has chosen a reminder for 7 p.m. At that time they are explaining a calculation to a friend. Consider three hypothetical responses:

1. The companion speaks immediately.
2. It shows a quiet indication that a reminder is waiting.
3. It remains silent because the person previously paused reminders.

Ask the participant which response they would want and why. Then change one detail: the person is now working alone. Does the answer change? Keep the reminder's purpose the same so the discussion stays focused on timing and presentation.

None of these options is automatically best. A visual indication might be missed; a spoken message might be unwelcome in a shared room. The useful result is a clearer account of the person's preference and circumstances.

## Make refusal easy to understand

Microsoft's dismissal guidance recommends making unwanted AI services easy to dismiss or ignore, including when a system activates unexpectedly. [Microsoft HAX: Support efficient dismissal](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/guideline/support-efficient-dismissal/).

For this companion concept, an interview could distinguish “dismiss this reminder,” “remind me later” and “pause reminders.” Ask what the person expects each action to do. A response that stops the current prompt but silently schedules another may not match their expectation.

These are interaction questions. They do not establish which buttons, sensors or voice controls a future device will contain.

## Evaluate acceptance without claiming better productivity

A simple concept session could use drawings or written scenarios. Ask participants to explain the proposed controls in their own words, identify an unwanted interruption and describe how they would stop it. Record misunderstandings and disagreements as well as positive reactions.

Liking a sketch is different from using a device over time. Such a discussion would not demonstrate improved grades, attention or productivity, and no such results are claimed here.

Think of one reminder you welcomed and one you ignored. What changed: timing, location, wording or your ability to postpone it? [Visit the NAVION ROBOTICS research page](https://navionrobotics.com/) to find the survey and share your perspective without including unnecessary personal information.
